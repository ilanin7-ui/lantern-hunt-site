1 · Direct removal

Every kill is one less lanternfly feeding on a vine or a tree.

Spotted lanternflies feed on the sap of more than 70 kinds of plants, including grapevines, hops, stone fruit trees, and hardwoods. The sticky, sugary fluid they excrete coats leaves, patios, and cars, and grows a black sooty mold that further damages the plants beneath it.

The damage is economic as well as ecological. A 2020 Penn State study estimated that if the insect spread across Pennsylvania it could cost the state at least $324 million a year and about 2,800 jobs, with grape growers, nurseries, Christmas tree farms, and forests among the hardest hit.

Removal is something anyone can do. An adult killed in late summer is one fewer insect feeding and laying eggs. An egg mass scraped off a tree in winter is 30 to 50 nymphs that never hatch. Lantern Hunt counts adults and egg masses separately and awards eight times the points for an egg mass, which gives participants a reason to seek out egg masses.

One person stopping to kill a few lanternflies on a walk is a small act. Hundreds of people doing it in the same weeks and the same neighborhoods add up to a coordinated control effort that no single agency could staff.

Source: Penn State, 2020; USDA APHIS.

2 · Education

People who learn to recognize it start removing it.

Most people have never been taught to recognize an invasive insect. Many walk past a spotted lanternfly, or a gray egg mass on a tree, without knowing what it is.

Lantern Hunt teaches what to look for at every stage: the gray egg masses of fall and winter, the black nymphs of late spring, the red nymphs of midsummer, and the spotted adults of late summer and fall. It explains when each stage appears, why the insect is a problem, and how to remove it.

That knowledge stays with people after they close the app. Someone who has learned to spot an egg mass notices egg masses on a neighbor’s tree, a park bench, or a trailer about to leave a quarantine area. They can tell friends, show their children, and report sightings to their state.

Invasive-species management is usually the work of specialists. Lantern Hunt turns it into something ordinary people understand and take part in.

Choosing species

Five tests for every species we take on.

Lantern Hunt does not ask people to kill anything unfamiliar or nonnative. It focuses many hands on a few harmful invasive species, chosen because ordinary people can make a real difference against them.

  1. 01

    Real harm

    The species causes meaningful ecological or agricultural damage.

  2. 02

    Safe and legal to remove

    Ordinary people can remove it safely and lawfully under the right conditions.

  3. 03

    Easy to identify

    It can be recognized reliably, with little risk of confusion with native species.

  4. 04

    Removal helps

    Individual removals contribute to control, such as eggs destroyed before they hatch or plants pulled before they seed.

  5. 05

    Verifiable

    A removal can be photographed and reviewed, which matters most once money is involved.

One approach, more species

The spotted lanternfly today. Five species that could come next.

The spotted lanternfly is what Lantern Hunt does now. These five pass the same tests and show how the approach could extend to plants, insects, and animals in other regions.

Operating nowInsect · 19 states and D.C.

Spotted lanternfly Lycorma delicatula

Feeds on grapevines, fruit trees, and hardwoods. Adults are conspicuous and egg masses scrape off by hand, which is why it was the first species. Participants have recorded {{STAT_VALUE_0}} verified kills so far, in six states.

Credit
PotentialPlant · Pacific Northwest

Scotch broom Cytisus scoparius

One shrub can produce more than 10,000 seeds a year, crowding out native plants and adding fuel for wildfire. Seedlings and small plants pull out by hand, roots and all, so every plant removed before it seeds keeps its seeds out of the soil.

Credit
PotentialInsect · Washington State

Japanese beetle Popillia japonica

Adults feed on more than 300 kinds of plants. Washington has been working to eradicate an infestation first found in Grandview in 2020. Beetles can be hand-picked into soapy water, so residents can help reduce numbers around homes and farms.

Credit
PotentialPlant · Eastern United States

Garlic mustard Alliaria petiolata

It disrupts the soil fungi that native plants depend on and crowds out spring wildflowers. Whole plants pull easily from April through June, and pulling them before they seed stops that year’s seed from reaching the soil.

Credit
PotentialInsect · Where established

Spongy moth egg masses Lymantria dispar

Its caterpillars feed on the leaves of forest and shade trees. An egg mass often holds more than 600 eggs, and scraping masses into soapy water from mid-October to mid-April reduces the next year’s caterpillars.

Credit
PotentialAnimal · Pacific Northwest

European green crab Carcinus maenas

Green crabs can prey on young oysters and clams and destroy the eelgrass beds and estuary habitat that salmon depend on, putting a shellfish industry worth more than $100 million a year at risk. Washington declared an emergency over the crab in 2022, and about 1.74 million were removed from its waters between January 2022 and March 2025. Larger, coordinated trapping efforts can help protect shellfish beds and coastal habitat.

Credit
